About Biu Emirate

A Land Rich in History and Purpose

The Biu Emirate is one of the oldest and most significant traditional institutions in Borno State, Nigeria. Founded in the mid-16th century by Yamta-ra-Wala, it has served as a pillar of governance, culture, and community identity for the Bura-Pabir people for nearly five centuries.

The Emirate encompasses diverse ethnic groups united by shared values — respect for leadership, deep Islamic faith, agricultural heritage, and a strong sense of community solidarity that transcends geography.

Chapter 01 — Origins

Origins & Early Settlements on the Biu Plateau

The story of the Biu people begins long before the formal establishment of the Kingdom. The fertile volcanic plateau that gives Biu its distinctive character has been home to human settlement for centuries. The Bura-Pabir people — whose origins oral tradition traces to migrations from Yemen through Sudan and the Lake Chad Basin — are among the earliest communities to make their homes on this elevated terrain in what is today Borno State, northeastern Nigeria.

The name Biu itself derives from the Babur and Bura word Viu, meaning "high" — a direct reference to the plateau's dominant elevation of 626 metres above the surrounding savannah plains. The high ground offered early settlers a natural strategic advantage: easily defensible terrain, volcanic soils enriched by ancient geological activity, and cooler temperatures ideal for permanent habitation and agriculture.

"The plateau was not merely where we lived — it was who we were. The land shaped us as surely as we shaped it."

— Oral tradition of the Pabir people, Biu Emirate

Yamtarawala, the founder of the Babur tribe and its kingdom, came to Biu from Birni Ngazargamu in the early period. He first settled in the Biu area at a place called Limbur, between Chikorkir and Mandaragirau, where he made his first capital. Trade routes connecting the Lake Chad basin passed through this region, bringing plateau communities into contact with broader networks of commerce and cultural exchange.

Chapter 02 — Founding

The Founding of the Biu Kingdom (~1535)

The rulers of Biu are numbered from Abdullahi, later known as Yamta-ra-Wala or Yamta the Great, who established his rule around 1535 CE. By conquest and by covenant, he unified the diverse communities of the plateau under a single political authority, founding what would become one of the enduring institutions of northeastern Nigeria.

Not until around 1670, in the reign of Mari Watila Tampta, did the kingdom become generally secure and widely recognised. The intervening century and a half saw successive rulers extend and consolidate the kingdom's territorial authority, resolve internal conflicts, and forge alliances that gave the Biu polity its lasting character.

The Structure of the Kingdom

From its earliest days, Biu was organised around the principle of the Mai — the king — as both political ruler and spiritual exemplar. The Pabir are the ruling class among the Bura. All Bura villages pay tribute to the ruler of Biu, and district heads approve the appointments of village heads. This layered governance system proved remarkably durable and continues to inform the Emirate's administrative structure today.

Founded by Yamta-ra-Wala ~1535 Secured ~1670 under Mari Watila Tampta Layered council governance Multi-ethnic coalition

A defining episode in the kingdom's history came during the reign of Mari Watirwa (1793 to 1838), who successfully defeated Fulani invaders from Gombe who attempted to subject Biu to their authority. This military victory preserved the kingdom's independence during a period when many neighbouring polities fell under Fulani suzerainty.

Chapter 03 — Faith

Islamic Faith & Scholarship in Biu

Islam arrived in the Biu area gradually through trade, migration, and the influence of Islamic scholars from across the Sahel and Sudan regions, long before the formal establishment of the Kingdom. The promotion of Islamic education was a cornerstone of the rulers' policy from the earliest days. Quranic schools were established across the plateau, attached to mosques and central to community life in both a spiritual and educational sense.

"Knowledge is the foundation upon which a just community is built. The rulers of Biu understood this from the beginning."

— Historical account of Islamic education in Borno, 19th century

Advanced Islamic scholarship — encompassing theology, jurisprudence, mathematics, and Arabic — was pursued by talented students who travelled to established centres of learning. Scholars of Biu origin contributed to the broader tradition of Islamic intellectual life in northern Nigeria. The legacy of this scholarly tradition is reflected in the high regard in which religious knowledge continues to be held in Biu communities today.

Chapter 04 — Land

The Biu Plateau & Its Geography

The Biu Plateau is one of Nigeria's most geologically distinctive regions — a basaltic volcanic plateau rising to 626 metres above the surrounding plains. Composed of basaltic lava flows with numerous cinder cones aligned over a fifty-kilometre belt, the plateau's dark, mineral-rich soils are among the most fertile in Borno State. Tila Hill, at 2,900 feet above sea level, is the highest point in the old Biu Division.

The volcanic heritage is visible across the landscape in extinct cones and craters, the most famous of which is Tila Lake — a sacred crater lake that has been a site of cultural and spiritual significance for generations. The Hawul River flows from the plateau into the River Gongola, a major tributary of the Benue. The Thirduldu Waterfalls at Vidau village is one of Nigeria's finest perennial waterfalls, less than an hour's drive from Biu town.

Climate and Agriculture

The elevated position gives the Biu Plateau a more temperate, semi-arid climate than the lowland regions of Borno. Millet, sorghum, maize, groundnuts, cowpea, and cotton are the staple crops, supplemented by livestock including cattle, goats, sheep, horses, and donkeys. Biu town is the chief trade centre for the plateau region. The plateau also supports a small mining industry extracting iron ore, gravel, magnesite, uranium, feldspar, topaz, mica, granite, and aquamarine.

Chapter 05 — Colonial Era

Colonial Era (1902–1960)

The arrival of British colonial administration in Borno at the dawn of the twentieth century brought profound changes to the Biu Kingdom. In 1878, Mari Biya became the first Bura king to rule from Biu town itself — a consolidation that positioned the town as the permanent seat of power. British rule was established in 1904, and the Biu Division was created in 1918.

In 1920, Mai Ari Dogo — King Ari the First, whose title was Kuthli Viyu — was acknowledged as the first Emir of Biu, formally adopting the Islamic title of Emir that aligned the institution with the broader Emirate system of northern Nigeria under British Indirect Rule.

"The Mai realized very soon that the British wished to centralize local administration. He used these goals to advance the power of Biu far beyond anything that had been possible in the pre-colonial era."

— Historical account citing colonial records

The British policy of Indirect Rule preserved the formal authority of the Emirate's rulers while subordinating it to colonial government. The most successful Emirs navigated this tension with skill — securing resources for their communities while preserving the cultural, religious, and social institutions that gave the Emirate its identity.

Chapter 06 — Independence

Independence & Modern Nigeria

Nigeria's independence on the first of October 1960 opened a new chapter for every community within it. The Biu Emirate today encompasses four Local Government Areas — Biu, Hawul, Kwaya Kusar, and Bayo — covering a significant portion of southern Borno State. By convention, the Biu Emirate has always produced the Deputy Governor of Borno State, representing the south of the state.

Borno State was created in 1976, and subsequent administrative reorganisations established Biu as the seat of both the Local Government Authority and the Emirate Council. Infrastructure investments during the oil-boom years transformed Biu town, connecting plateau communities to state and national networks and enabling the rise of a generation of Biu citizens in medicine, law, the civil service, academia, and the military.

Chapter 07 — Sacred Sites

Tila Lake & The Sacred Sites of Biu

Among the many remarkable features of the Biu Plateau, Tila Lake stands apart. This pristine crater lake is nestled within an extinct volcanic cone measuring approximately 550 metres across. The crocodiles of Tila Lake are sacred to Biu's traditional rulers, as confirmed by the Encyclopaedia Britannica. They are believed to be the spiritual guardians of the Emirate, whose wellbeing is symbolically linked to the fortunes of the kingdom.

"The crocodiles of Tila Lake are sacred to Biu's traditional rulers."

— Encyclopædia Britannica, "Biu" entry

These crocodiles are said to be of a creamier colour than those in surrounding rivers. Uniquely, nobody has determined what they feed on, as there are no fish or frogs in the lake. The crocodile is the symbol used to represent the Biu Kingdom. Tila Hill, overlooking the lake at 2,900 feet, is the highest point in the old Biu Division. The Thirduldu Waterfalls at Vidau village and the Kwadankir Pool are additional sites of cultural and tourist significance in the Biu area.

Chapter 08 — People & Culture

People, Language & Culture

The Biu Emirate is home primarily to the Bura-Pabir people — also called Babur — a Chadic-speaking community within the Afro-Asiatic language family whose origins are traced through Sudan and the Chad Basin. The Pabir are the ruling class; the Bura people form the majority of the population across the Emirate's villages and communities. Hausa, as the dominant language of northern Nigerian commerce, is widely understood. Arabic occupies an important place in religious and scholarly life.

Bura-Pabir — Chadic / Afro-Asiatic Hausa widely spoken Sallah Durbar tradition Griot oral tradition

Cultural expression takes many forms — from the elaborate Sallah Durbar, when the Emir receives the homage of his chiefs on horseback in a tradition of breathtaking colour and pageantry, to the music, storytelling, and poetry that animate everyday community life. Griots — traditional praise-singers and oral historians — serve as living archives of the Emirate's history, genealogies, and values.

Chapter 09 — Economy

Economy & Development Through the Ages

Agriculture has been the bedrock of the Biu economy across all periods of its history. The Local Government Area is mostly located in the northern Guinea savannah agroecological zone. The economy is mixed agricultural — based on herding cattle, goats, sheep, horses, and donkeys, and farming sorghum, millet, maize, cowpea, and cotton. Biu town hosts one of the largest periodic markets in the sub-region.

There is also a small but notable mining industry in Biu, with iron ore, gravel, magnesite, uranium, feldspar, topaz, mica, granite, aquamarine, nephelite, and salt being extracted. The modern economy encompasses a substantial civil service workforce, a College of Education, and a growing business sector in retail, hospitality, and transport.

Chapter 10 — The Future

Biu Today & Into the Future

The Biu of today is a community in proud possession of a rich history, navigating the challenges and opportunities of the twenty-first century with the same qualities that have always defined its people — faith, resilience, solidarity, and an abiding commitment to collective welfare.

"We are not just preserving our past — we are building our future. And we are building it together, as we always have."

— Ahmad Abdullahi, Director, Biu Forum · 2026

The establishment of the Biu Forum as the community's official digital platform affirms that the ties of belonging that have always held Biu together are not diminished by distance or the passage of time. The history of Biu is not a closed book — it is being written, with every passing year, by the people of the Emirate themselves.
